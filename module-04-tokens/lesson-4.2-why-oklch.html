Lesson 4.2

Why OKLCH

Every colour system before OKLCH lies to you in subtle ways — about brightness, about hue, about what "same darkness" means. OKLCH is the first colour model in CSS where the numbers match what your eyes actually see.

The problems you already have

Hex and RGB: three channels of red, green, and blue light. No relationship to human perception. #808080 and #ff0000 are both "50%" in some channel, but one is medium gray and the other is fully saturated red. You cannot reason about lightness or saturation from the numbers. You have to pick with a colour picker and hope.

HSL: a step up. You get hue, saturation, and lightness — but the "lightness" is wrong. hsl(240 100% 50%) (blue) and hsl(60 100% 50%) (yellow) are both "50% lightness" on paper, but the yellow is visually far brighter. Build a palette by stepping the H channel while keeping L constant and you get a stripe that gets dramatically lighter and darker across the spectrum. HSL is perceptually uneven.

What OKLCH is

OKLCH has three components that actually mean what you think they mean:

  • L — Lightness. A percentage from 0% (black) to 100% (white). Two colours with the same L look equally bright, regardless of hue. This is the property HSL fakes and OKLCH gets right.
  • C — Chroma. How saturated the colour is, roughly 0 to 0.4. Zero is gray; higher is more vivid. Unlike HSL's "saturation," C is bounded differently per hue and lightness — so you can exceed the displayable gamut if you push too far. That is a feature: it tells you when a colour is physically impossible to show.
  • H — Hue. An angle from 0 to 360 degrees. 0 is pinkish-red, 90 is yellow-green, 180 is teal, 270 is blue-purple. Stepping the hue with L and C fixed produces a perceptually uniform colour wheel.

Written: oklch(55% 0.22 290) → 55% lightness, chroma 0.22, hue 290 (purple).

Perceptual uniformity, demonstrated

The practical consequence: you can build a palette by picking one L and one C, then stepping H around the wheel, and every colour looks like it came from the same family. Below, every swatch has L = 65%, C = 0.17, and only H changes. Compare the visual evenness to what you would get from stepping HSL — where the yellow would scorch your eyes and the blue would sink into the background.

Practical reasons PE7 standardises on it

  • Predictable scales. A 9-stop scale from L = 95% down to L = 15% produces visually even steps you can design against.
  • Accessible text contrast. Because L is perceptually correct, you can pick text colours by lightness difference and trust the result.
  • Dark mode for free. Invert L across your palette and the result actually looks like the same brand in the dark.
  • Wide-gamut ready. OKLCH can express colours outside sRGB, so P3-display hardware shows you colours RGB syntax cannot reach.

From Module 4 onward, every colour in the course is written in OKLCH. Hex and HSL appear only as counter-examples.

Mini-exercise

Rewrite every swatch in HSL instead of OKLCH, keeping the same lightness and saturation (hsl(H 60% 60%) is a reasonable starting point). Run it. Identify which swatches look dramatically out of family with the others — that visual unevenness is exactly what OKLCH fixes.
